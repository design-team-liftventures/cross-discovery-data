Reaching Book Club Members Who Don't Know Us
Cross-interview synthesis · "BCMs Outside SuperSummary" · July 2026
8 interviews · Lead interviewer: Kaitlyn Hurley · Analysis focus: awareness & acquisition, book club jobs-to-be-done, barriers & objections




The one-paragraph version

Book club members are already doing the jobs SuperSummary was built for — they are just doing
them with Google, ChatGPT, Reddit, Pinterest and their own memory of high-school English. The
problem is not that SuperSummary lacks the content; in several cases the content has already
reached these people and left no trace. It is that SuperSummary is invisible at the two moments
clubs actually feel pain (picking the next book and arriving able to talk about it), it is absent
from the surfaces clubs actually live on (Instagram, Slack, iMessage, WhatsApp, Facebook
groups), and its academic inheritance actively repels a culture that defines itself as not school. The
fastest path in is not a new app — it is (1) a shareable, club-shaped artifact that a leader can paste
into the channel she already runs, and (2) re-pointing existing assets at the “book club is Thursday
and I didn’t finish it” moment.

The interviewer’s own read is worth quoting because it framed the study:


     “every single interview I’ve done so far, no one has heard of Super Summary. And I was like, I
     think that’s actually better. Like I think it actually gives us more information on like how do we
     find new people.” — interviewer, Veronika session


But the sharper finding is the bottom half of that table. Three of eight had already touched the
product — one had an account. None of them has a relationship with the brand. That reframes the
problem:


     This is not (only) a reach problem. It is an attribution and activation problem.


Jen states the attribution gap in one line, on hearing the name for the first time:


     “I wonder if I have used it before for like book club questions and just didn’t know what it was.”


If the guides are already ranking for book-club intent and delivering value anonymously, the highest-
leverage work is not more awareness spend — it is capturing the visit that already happens.


1.2 The one proven organic door is “book club discussion questions” via Google — and it leaks
Both participants who found SuperSummary on their own found it the same way, and both were
hosts in a failure state.

Rashida:


     “I was trying to come up with book club questions and I did a Google search and it led me to
     your page.”


And the reason she was searching:

     “the reason I use super summaries, because that’s the book I did not like, so I didn’t finish it. So
     I could not come up with questions on my own.”


Jessica, same door, same job, no account at all:


     “what I do is just to look at like what other questions you guys are suggesting to ask or like
     something that I may have not thought of while I was crafting out questions… I don’t think I have
     an official account, to be honest.”


Three things follow.

1. Acquisition today is failure-triggered, not exploration-driven. The trigger is a host who
     cannot write questions — usually because she didn’t finish or didn’t like the book. That is a
     narrow, episodic need. Once resolved, the need disappears and so does the user. Rashida is the
     documented case: arrive → extract one artifact → never return.
2. The landing experience does nothing to widen the visit. Rashida left without knowing
     anything else existed. Jessica evaluated the entire platform on the one artifact she happened to
     land on, and it was the weakest one for her use case (more on that in 3.6) — while noticing,
     almost in passing, that the summaries were better than she expected: “I do like the summary.
     Like, you guys summarizing stuff that was pretty cool.”
3. The door is host-shaped. Members do not search for discussion questions. Members search —
     when they search at all — for recall.


1.3 Discovery in this segment travels through people, not platforms
Nobody in this sample discovered a reading tool through advertising or app-store browsing. Every
mechanism named is social.

Trusted-taste chains. Veronika describes the entire discovery pipeline for her personal reading:


     “someone I whose taste I trust reads a book, they tell me to read it and then I talk to them about
     it, and then if I tell someone else to it and they read it, then we talk about it”


And her acquisition channel is a curated bookseller email, not a ratings site: “because it’s through
my local bookstore, they’ll do emails and they’re like, hey, bookseller recommended things.”

Club fission from a single organizer. Lisa belongs to three clubs — all three trace to one woman
she met at an apartment-complex event. One organizer generated three clubs, three formats
(monthly / seasonal / weekly-serialized) and the entire membership of each, within months.

Referral offered unprompted, within minutes. Julia volunteered two referrals during her call
(including Savannah, who became interview #7). Savannah, minutes after hearing the brand name
for the first time, offered to poll her four-person group chat, send screenshots of all four responses,
and recruit a friend from an adjacent club. Lisa offered to text her organizer.


     Implication: the acquisition unit is not a user. It is a club. And the entry point into a club is one
     person — the organizer, or the one member who does logistics.

1.6 The competitive set is free, general-purpose, and already open in another tab
Nobody named a paid book club product as an alternative. What they named:

     Google → Rashida’s and Jessica’s entry path
     Reddit → Julia’s default recall aid
     SparkNotes → Lisa’s one prior use of a study guide (a competitor whose equity is academic —
     she reached for the school brand because that is where the category is filed in her head)
     ChatGPT / Gemini → Lisa for structuring her notes; Celeste for questions (“Some may come
     from Chad”); two of Veronika’s colleagues for mid-read catch-up
     Goodreads reviews → Veronika, retrospectively, to find disagreement to fuel discussion
     Pinterest / BuzzFeed Tasty → Lisa for themed food

Celeste is the clearest statement of why awareness has not converted to trial: her free, improvised
stack is good enough.


     “I’ll do a little bit of everything, so might come up myself. Some I look up online that are already
     available. Some may come from Chad.”


Sign-up therefore requires a capability her improvised stack cannot produce — not a nicer version
of what it already produces.

The most important competitive datapoint in the study is Veronika describing her colleagues:


     “she will ask chat GPT what should I know at this point? And she’ll say how many pages you
     read? Like in case she’s like a little lost… And then similarly like if I liked this book, what else
     should I. What else would I like?”


Those are Story Recap and recommendations. SuperSummary ships both. The user reinvented them
in a chat prompt because she had no idea they existed. The competitor for SuperSummary’s
best features is a blank chat box. The counter-argument SuperSummary has is expert-vetted
accuracy — and Jessica, who cares most about accuracy, is the leader most likely to value it.

The club lifecycle, and where the pain actually sits. Note how little of it is “help me understand this
book.”

Job 1 — Pick the next book (named as the highest-friction job)
Jen opened and closed her interview on this, unprompted:


     “how we choose our books is something that, like, from the sounds of it would be something like,
     great to explore because I think that’s where we spend like the most time kind of going back and
     forth” … “where I think the most value would be, at least for my group, is, like, easy picking our
     next book.”


Selection is where every club has a ritual but no method:

     Celeste: two-round funnel — open nominations (15–20 titles) → volunteer committee shortlists 4
     → community poll. The committee’s only quality signal is a Goodreads proxy: “We try to never
     read a book that’s below, like, a 3.5 rating on Goodreads.”
     Savannah: ~26 titles in a shared note, pasted into Wheel of Names and spun. “it’s like
     gibberish. Like, half the time we don’t even remember what the book is, how it got on there.”
     Veronika: the picker posts three candidates to Slack; ~20 vote; ~3 read.
     Rashida: picks alone, deliberately choosing books she has not read — absorbing all the risk.
     Julia / Jen / Savannah: all three have a no-repeats rule — Julia’s is the strictest (“it has to be a
     book that no one at the table has read, which is really hard because there’s like, 15 of us, and
     they’re all really avid readers”), and she notes the perverse effect: “we’ve been reading obscure
     books that are, like, outside of anyone’s genre of interest, because if they were within the genre
     of interest, then you would have read them already.”

Four distinct sub-jobs fall out, and every one of them is a place SuperSummary content could sit:

1a. Know what’s actually in the book before committing. This is the most under-served and
highest-stakes need in the study. Savannah:


     “we’re not usually in the habit of reading the synopsis of the book ahead of time”


Which produced the horror incident — Sarah picked The Southern Book Club’s Guide to Vampire
Slaying off a black-and-white Kindle thumbnail with peaches and the word “Southern” on it:


     “Jamie and I start reading it and are having nightmares… my husband, like, looked at my Kindle
     and saw what I was reading, and he goes, are you reading horror? And I said, no one told me it
     was a horror book.”


She explicitly assessed this as a potential club-ending event: “this is either going to destroy this
book club or we’ll laugh about it and bounce back.” Veronika’s club hit the same wall from the other
direction — The Man Who Died Nine Times had content several members would not read past:
“there’s a lot of people like couldn’t get past the incest. I’m like, yeah, this discussion topic we’re
going to bring.” The content became the discussion by default.

A spoiler-free tone/content/intensity signal at the pick moment is a club-cohesion feature, not a
discovery feature. Book Briefs are the closest existing asset and neither person knew it existed.

1b. Know how long it will take. Jen, explicitly:


     “even honestly give, like, oh, it’s 270 pages, like, average reading time or something like that.”

Grounded in a real exclusion: “[Yesteryear] I didn’t think was long, but a lot of people in the group
were like, yeah, I’m like eight months pregnant. I’m not. I don’t have time to read a book like that.
And our next one’s like 215 pages or something.” Page count is a feasibility filter tied to life stage,
not enrichment. Jessica is planning the opposite direction — 500+ page epics — and needs the same
signal.

1c. Know whether it will sustain a two-hour conversation. Celeste’s committee uses a 3.5
Goodreads rating as a proxy because no discussion-quality signal exists anywhere.

1d. Know who in the club already read it / wants to read it. Jen already reverse-engineers this
manually in Goodreads and named the pattern she wants, from a restaurant app:


     “if you go to a restaurant’s page, any of your mutuals, it’ll be like, they also have marked it as
     want to try. So it’s like, I feel like as simple as that. It’s like, oh, like three of my friends have also
     marked interest in reading this book.”



Job 2 — Survive the gap between picking and getting the book
This is a structural, months-long dead zone that nobody serves. Julia:


     “we put our book for July on hold in March because it was such a long wait. And then it was, you
     know, towards the end of last month in June, when I put on my Instagram story, does anybody
     have this book? Because I need to read it by July 16, and I am still, like, 400th in line for this.”


She sourced a physical copy from an Instagram follower and ended up with six days to read a book
she normally takes a month to read. Savannah’s club is structurally reorganized around the same
problem:


     “we usually request all of our books from Libby. And if we pick a book that’s too popular and has
     like a three or four month wait, then in the months we are waiting for the book, we either talk
     about the other books we’ve read and we play Moshe during it.”


Two openings here: (a) something to do during the wait, and (b) instant availability as a competitive
claim against a hold queue. Also worth noting — Savannah’s club holds meetings with no shared
book, which is a content need SuperSummary has no answer for at all (cross-book / thematic-bridge
conversation).


Job 3 — Show up able to talk (not: understand the book)
This is the job SuperSummary is closest to already owning, and it has two completely different
triggers that need different framing.

Trigger A — Didn’t finish. Universal and, in most of these clubs, socially fine:

     Rashida’s meetings literally open with it: “My first question of every meeting is, who didn’t finish
     the book?” — and she answers it herself too.
     Jen: “People come whether they read the book or not or whether they’ve gotten, like, halfway
     through it. And they’re just like, I’m open to spoilers. So it’s kind of an agreement.”
     Savannah: “If someone doesn’t read the book, we don’t kick anyone out, but we do roast them for
     not finishing the book.” (She quit Intermezzo at 40 pages and still went.)

     Celeste names the audience herself: “this could definitely be helpful for the girlies who maybe
     that month they didn’t have time to read the full book, but still want to be part of the
     conversation.”
     Celeste is also in this group as the host: “there’s times where I do read the books, there’s times
     where I don’t finish the books” — because running four events a month crowds it out.
     Veronika’s club: ~20 vote, ~3 read.

And the single most valuable quote in the study, from Julia, who skipped a meeting she wanted to
attend:


     “But I could have had the community this week, but I missed it because I didn’t read the book.
     And maybe an app or a summary or something would have helped me feel better about going.”


Note why she skipped — not comprehension. Spoiler risk plus a felt breach of obligation:


     “My end of the bargain is that I will read this book and I will show up ready to participate in the
     discussion. And I left the book club.”


This means catch-up content protects attendance, which protects the club. That is a much
bigger claim than “helps you keep up.”

Trigger B — Finished, but forgot. Equally common and completely unaddressed:

     Jen finished Yesteryear within a week of selection and discussed it six weeks later: “I remember
     liking it. I remember like having some like, whatever feelings about it, but I was like, I couldn’t
     tell you, like, really in depth how I felt about it until we were actually talking about it.” The cause
     is schedule lag, not weak reading — and because the meeting date is known in advance, this
     need is predictable and schedulable.
     Julia’s club includes a librarian who “reads like a book a day… she’ll forget what the book was
     even about by the time we meet.”
     Lisa’s one prior study-guide use was recall, not catch-up: “I hadn’t read the book in, like, five
     years.”
     Julia already does spoiler-bounded recall manually, switching between print and audio: “I went on
     airplane mode in my brain and I did not listen to that chapter. So now I’m gonna read it.”

Story Recap maps onto Trigger B almost perfectly. Nobody knew it existed.


Job 4 — Prep the meeting, if you’re the picker or the host
In Lisa’s clubs there is an unwritten rule: choosing the book means owning the discussion.


     “my assumption is the expectation is if you pick the book, you are also signing up to moderate
     and lead the conversation”


And the one meeting she reports as a failure failed at exactly that handoff — the picker prepped
nothing and the organizer had to cover. This is a precise trigger moment: the obligation is created at
the moment of selection, weeks before it is felt.

Same pattern elsewhere: Jessica’s Discord goes seasonally dead (“kind of like, dead” in summer);

solves the leak (reactions discharged over text before the meeting) and the honesty problem (author
visibility) in one move — and it’s a thing no tracker offers, so it doesn’t compete with Goodreads.




Part 3 — Barriers & Objections


3.1 The academic inheritance is an active repellent, not neutral baggage
This is the positioning barrier, and it is stated as a design principle by the most experienced leader
in the sample. Jessica, on why her meetings are deliberately loose:


     “I don’t like that environment. I don’t want it to feel like we’re in school.”


Her reason is retention: “I always find that when people join book clubs, they’re always intimidated.
Intimidated that when they’re discussing books that they’ll feel stupid or something is a stupid
question.” Attendance is protected by removing evaluation, not adding rigor.

Corroborating:

     Savannah on formal clubs: “it feels like a chore to me.” She refuses to join her friend Lauren’s
     12-person club where “if you haven’t read the book, you’re not allowed to go” — she calls it
     “straight up terrifying.”
     Celeste’s club identity: “half girls night out, half book club.”




BCMs Outside SuperSummary · Cross-interview synthesis · July 2026                                    Page 10 / 16
     Lisa reached for SparkNotes — the school brand — because that’s where the whole category is
     filed mentally.


     Direct consequence: quizzes, comprehension scoring, and essay prompts are not just low-
     priority for this segment — they are counter-signals. Anything that reads as assessment will be
     filtered out before it is evaluated. This is the single largest gap between SuperSummary’s
     current Plus feature set and this audience’s culture.



3.2 App fatigue and logging redundancy — the near-universal veto
Every participant who discussed reading apps described the same wall.

     Savannah, on why Fable failed with her club: “I already logged my books in Goodreads and
     Storygraph. I don’t need one more app.” She also couldn’t tell what Fable was: “we didn’t
     understand if we were supposed to like read our books on Fable or if it was really supposed to be
     more like storygraph.”
     Jen: “it just already exists with all my information. So unfortunately, I’m just, like, not willing to
     spend the time to move all of my books over… But I liked Storygraph when I tried it.” She liked
     the better product and stayed anyway.
     Julia: same — opened a StoryGraph account, abandoned it, because Goodreads auto-logs from
     Kindle/Libby and everyone else is there.
     Celeste is a lapsed tracker entirely, because she rotates between audio, e-book and print: “I’m
     not the best at keep like staying on top of Goodreads.”

Two hard rules fall out:

1. Preference and retention are decoupled in this category. Differentiating on interface quality
   will not move anyone. (The interviewer reached the same conclusion in-session, repeatedly: “I
     don’t want to be a prettier Goodreads.”)
2. Never depend on the user having logged their progress. Celeste and Julia won’t have. Any
   progress-aware feature needs a zero-friction input — “what chapter are you on?” — not a synced
     library.


3.3 Host labor is the gate on every leader-side feature
Rashida declined a feature her own members requested because it added maintenance. That is
the clearest possible statement of the constraint: member demand does not translate into host
adoption unless the feature removes work.

Same signal from Jessica, who runs selection, prep, moderation, community management, two
platforms, side quests, author brunches, audiobook walks and travel retreats — alone. Her scarce
resource is time, not comprehension. Positioning SuperSummary to her as a way to understand
books better addresses a need she does not have.


3.4 Willingness to pay is weak, and in places principled
     Lisa: “I hate buying books.” She borrows via Libby and uses Libro.fm to route around Amazon. A
     subscription that replaces neither the book nor the purchase has to justify itself against someone
     who declines to pay for the books.
     Jessica refuses to charge for her club on principle: “I don’t necessarily believe in charging
     people for something that could honestly be free.” She monetizes adjacent premium experiences



BCMs Outside SuperSummary · Cross-interview synthesis · July 2026                                     Page 11 / 16
     instead. A personal subscription works directly against her stated economics; value delivered to
     her members or to her credibility as a curator is the tradeable currency.
     Rashida: “this is not making me any money and I work and raise kids.” She rejected her app’s

     to attend.



3.5 “Isn’t reading supposed to be a break from my phone?”
Julia’s objection, worth taking seriously because she works in community building:


     “the idea of having an app is, like, hard for me to grasp because I’m like, aren’t we reading books
     because we, like, want a break from, like, our phones? And this gives us the opportunity to be
     real, present with people.”


She resolves it herself, and the resolution is the design instruction: value belongs before and after
the meeting, not during it. In-meeting phone activities will meet resistance from this profile.
(Notably, Celeste’s in-meeting mechanics are deliberately analog — printed PDFs, cards, dice — with
the QR code as backup.)


3.6 Content register is a real quality objection, not just a discovery gap
Two independent, unprompted complaints about the questions themselves:

     Jessica: “they have one or two questions that I like” per guide — and she still writes her own set
     from scratch. She evaluated the whole platform on this one artifact.
     Savannah: “I think discussion questions would actually be very helpful” — paired with consistent
     abandonment: “Every time we try it, we’ll do like the first two questions and then just kind of like
     go, oh, okay. And then what about this part?”

The failure isn’t that prompts don’t exist. It’s the shape: a sequential list to work through, versus
fuel for the overlapping, tangential conversation these groups actually have. Compare what actually
works in the room:

     Jessica’s real opener: “how did everybody feel about the book?” — reaction before analysis, and it
     determines where she goes next.
     Jen’s club: a five-star rating plus a short rationale from each person.
     Veronika deliberately hunted Goodreads for disagreement to seed conversation: “I wanted the
     look at reviews to see if… other people were conflicted. And so that was a discussion point.”
     Celeste’s questions serve social onboarding, not literary analysis: “the people who are using
     those questions are mainly, like, newer girlies who obviously don’t know anybody. So that’s like,
     the perfect kind of, like, icebreaker.”

3.7 Genre relevance
Julia, on her club’s actual appetite (contemporary romance and “smut”), and what the analytical
apparatus is worth against it — readers of those books, she says, don’t “care about the Character’s
development that much.” Meanwhile Lisa’s erotic-fiction club read Lights Out, which is on
SuperSummary — and she had no idea. Coverage exists; perceived relevance doesn’t.


3.8 Not-reading is sanctioned socially — but reading is moralized individually
The one place to be careful. Rashida, Jen and Savannah all confirm non-completion carries no real
penalty. But Julia frames finishing as a bargain she owes the group, and left an earlier club rather
than under-deliver: “I don’t read at this pace. Sorry.”


     Positioning must read as “participate fully”, never “skip the book.” An offer framed as a
     substitute for reading collides head-on with this self-concept — in the very person who
     articulated the strongest acquisition trigger in the study.




Part 4 — How SuperSummary Gets In

Ordered by leverage against the awareness problem, with the evidence each rests on and what has
to be true.


Tier 1 — Do these first
1. Fix the door that already exists: capture and widen the “book club questions” search
visit. Two of eight found SuperSummary through this exact query. Both left with one artifact.
Rashida had an account and never came back; Jessica never made one. Before spending anything on
new awareness, make that landing experience (a) show a host what else is there — Book Brief,
chapter summaries, recipes, reading schedule — and (b) capture at the actual moment of need, mid-
prep. This is a conversion and merchandising fix, not a build. Evidence: Rashida, Jessica, Jen’s
attribution gap. Lowest cost, highest certainty in this list.

2. Re-point existing assets at “book club is Thursday and I didn’t finish it” — and frame it
as attendance, not comprehension. Study guides, chapter summaries, Story Recap and audio
narration already do this job. The gap is entirely framing and discovery. The claim to make is Julia’s,
in her words: you don’t have to miss the night. Guardrail from 3.8: participate fully, never skip the
book. Guardrail from 3.5: pre-meeting, not in-meeting. Evidence: Julia (skipped a meeting she
wanted), Celeste (names the audience herself, and is in it), Savannah, Jen, Rashida’s opening
question, Veronika’s 20-vote/3-read funnel. No new features required.

3. Get Book Briefs to the moment a club picks a book — as a cohesion signal, not a
discovery feature. Blind selection is universal here, and it has already nearly broken one club
(Savannah’s horror incident) and hijacked another’s discussion (Veronika’s). Add the three signals

people actually asked for: tone/content intensity (spoiler-free), length + average reading time
(Jen’s explicit request, Jessica’s 500+-page plans), and some read on whether it will sustain a
discussion (Celeste’s committee is using a 3.5 Goodreads rating as a stand-in today). Evidence:
Savannah, Veronika, Jen, Celeste, Rashida picking blind. Content largely exists; the work is

4. Ship artifacts into the channels clubs already run, and make leaders the distribution.
Nobody will move their club to a new destination. But Celeste hand-builds a printable multi-page
PDF and a QR page every month; Rashida hand-builds a reading schedule for Instagram Stories

seeding the interviewer already improvised — free premium to leaders, in exchange for nothing but
usage. Evidence: Celeste, Rashida, Jessica’s reach; Veronika’s Slack-only club; the unprompted
referrals from Julia, Savannah and Lisa. Constraint: must reduce host labor, per 3.3.


Tier 2 — Strong evidence, more build
5. Reshape Book Club Questions from a list into openers. Reaction first, disagreement second,
comparison to other reads third; selectable, reorderable, editable. This is a genuine quality gap,
independently reported by the two people who used the questions most. Evidence: Jessica (1–2
usable per guide), Savannah (abandons after two), Celeste (icebreaker function), Jessica’s actual
opener.

6. Reading-schedule generator: book + meeting date → weekly chapter blocks. Rashida does
this by hand every cycle; Lisa’s weekly club needs the member-side version and already read a
guide’s chapter structure as matching her cadence. Directly removes host labor, which is the only
kind of leader feature that gets adopted. Evidence: Rashida, Lisa.

7. A private club record — with ratings hidden until the meeting. The only unprompted
feature request in the study, and it solves three problems at once: the pre-meeting reaction leak, the
author-visibility honesty problem, and the total absence of club memory. It complements Goodreads
rather than competing with it, which is the only way past the 3.2 veto. Evidence: Savannah (explicit
request + manual workaround), Jen (4 years of ratings + rationales lost in a group text), Rashida
(won’t collect data that doesn’t close a loop).

8. Serve the hold-queue interval. Months elapse between picking and getting the book, and
Savannah’s club holds bookless meetings to cover it. Two plays: something to do during the wait,
and instant availability as a claim against a 400-person queue. Also the one context where “what do
we talk about with no shared book” becomes a content need. Evidence: Julia (March hold for a July
meeting, 400th in line, 6-day read), Savannah (3–4 month holds, meets anyway).

Part 5 — What This Study Can’t Tell You

Gaps worth closing before betting real roadmap on any of the above:

1. No pricing or value-perception testing happened. Zero participants were asked what they’d
   pay. Every “I’d use that” in these transcripts followed either a feature pitch or a free-subscription
     offer.
2. Members are under-sampled relative to leaders. Only three pure members (Julia, Jen,
   Veronika), and Veronika’s club is an HR initiative, not a self-organized one. The 3HAG target —
     the daily-use book club member — is thin here.
3. No non-millennial, non-US, or male participants. No older clubs, no library-affiliated clubs,
   no church or school clubs, no clubs where the book is the point.
4. The clubs that died are the most instructive and least explored. Savannah has lost two
   (Zoom fatigue, life-stage mismatch); Jessica sat alone in Zoom rooms for years. Retention
   research on failed clubs would say more about what a book club platform must do than another
     interview with a healthy club.
5. The strongest unprompted acquisition trigger — Julia’s skipped meeting — is a sample
   of one. It should be the first thing tested, in a message test rather than another interview.
6. Nobody was shown the product and observed using it. Every judgment of SuperSummary’s
     content quality here (Jessica’s “one or two questions”) comes from unaided recall of a past visit.
