Book Clubs Concept 2 — Usability Test Results
Sep 17, 2026 · @Design Team
Overview
This report summarizes the results of a Lyssna first-click usability test for [Book Clubs] Concept 2, a Figma prototype of a book club app. The study is complete, with 15 participants recruited from a panel (Panel order 2), all located in the United States.
Participant mix: 10 female, 5 male; skewed toward full-time employed (11), with some part-time (2) and retired (2); education ranged from high school graduate through postgraduate, with the largest groups holding some college (4) or a college degree (5); household income was concentrated in the $40,001–$70,000 range (6 of 15); technical proficiency was intermediate (8) to advanced (7). All 15 participants listed reading as a hobby/interest, consistent with the study's target audience.
Participants completed five first-click tasks against the prototype. Each task was followed by three questions: whether they found what they were looking for, how easy or difficult it was, and whether the target was where they expected it to be.
Task 1: Finding book details
Prompt: "You're curious about the book itself — the author, the background, what it's about. Where would you tap first to dig into the book?"
This was the strongest-performing task: every participant reached the goal screen, and all reported finding what they needed exactly or roughly where expected. This is the clearest "win" pattern in the study.
Task 2: Setting up a vote for the next book
Prompt: "Your club needs to pick the next book. You want everyone to vote on it. Where would you tap first to set that up?"
One participant did not reach the goal screen, and misclick rates were the second-highest in the study (14%). No one reported the target being somewhere they did not expect at all, but over half only found it "roughly" where expected — suggesting the entry point for starting a vote is discoverable but not fully obvious.
Task 3: Preparing to discuss the book
Prompt: "Your club meets tomorrow night. You want to walk in ready to talk about the book. Where would you tap first?"
This task shows the weakest follow-up sentiment in the study: 20% said they did not find what they were looking for, and another 20% said the target was not where they expected. This is a signal worth investigating — participants can technically reach a goal screen while still feeling the content that helps them "prepare to discuss" isn't where they'd look.
Task 4: Reacting to a hot take
Prompt: "Find the section where members share their hottest opinions about the book, and react to one of them."
This task has by far the highest misclick rate in the study (29%), the lowest ease score (4.07), and one participant rated it "very difficult" (the only 1-rating recorded anywhere in the test). Locating the opinions/reactions section appears to be the hardest navigation step of the five tasks, even though most participants eventually succeeded.
Task 5: Looking up a past book
Prompt: "You're trying to remember the name of a book your club read a few months ago. Where would you tap first to look it up?"
This is the best-performing task in the entire study across every metric: full success, near-zero misclicks, the fastest average time, and a perfect "exactly where expected" score. A reading-history or past-books feature appears to be highly intuitive in this prototype.
Summary and recommendations
Across all five tasks, participants generally located the intended feature (93–100% success), and no task scored below a mean ease of 4.0 on a 5-point scale — overall this concept tests well for basic discoverability.
Two areas stand out for design attention before this concept moves forward:
Reacting to a hot take (Task 4) had the highest misclick rate (29%) and lowest ease score (4.07) in the study, including the only "very difficult" rating recorded. The entry point to the opinions/reactions section is likely not visually prominent enough or is competing with other navigation targets.
Preparing to discuss the book (Task 3) had the weakest "found what they were looking for" result (80% Yes) and the most participants saying the target was not where expected (20%). Even though most participants technically reached a goal screen, this suggests the discussion-prep content itself may not be framed the way users expect when they think about "getting ready to talk about the book."
By contrast, looking up a past book (Task 5) and finding book details (Task 1) were the standout successes, with near-perfect scores across every metric — these patterns are good reference points for how to structure the weaker flows.
